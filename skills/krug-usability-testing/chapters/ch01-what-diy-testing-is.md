# Chapter 1: You don't see any elephants around here, do you?
*(includes the Opening Remarks)*

## Core Idea
Usability testing means **watching people try to use what you're building**, and the cheap, informal, qualitative kind works every time: all products have problems, the serious ones are easy to find, and watching makes the team better designers.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Usability testing (definition)**: "Watching people try to use what you're creating/designing/building… with the intention of (a) making it easier for people to use or (b) proving that it is easy to use."
  - The defining element is **actual use**. That separates it from surveys, interviews, and focus groups, which ask people for *opinions* or *past experiences*.
- **Quantitative vs. qualitative testing**:
  - **Quantitative** sets out to *prove* something ("Is v2 better than v1?"). It measures success rate and time-on-task. It needs a fixed protocol, careful data collection, a statistically significant sample, and representative users. The facilitator interacts as little as possible, and the extreme form is "Voice of God" testing.
  - **Qualitative / do-it-yourself** sets out to get *insights* that let you **improve** the thing. It can be unscientific. You can test few users and **change the protocol mid-test**, for example by rewriting or skipping a task after participant 1 fails it for an obvious reason.
- **The DIY test format**: a facilitator sits with one participant, gives tasks, and asks them to **think out loud**. There is no data gathering. The team watches from another room via screen sharing, then debriefs and decides what to fix.
- **Why it always works**, in three reasons:
  1. **All sites have problems.** Mature, heavily tested products included.
  2. **Most serious problems are easy to find.** They sit in plain sight. You can't see them because *you know how it's supposed to work*.
  3. **Watching users makes you a better designer.** Without it, teams design "for our abstract idea of users, based for the most part on ourselves."
- **Division of labor (after Jakob Nielsen)**: everybody should do simple user testing, which amounts to debugging a design. Professionals do quantitative, comparative, and new-technology testing. Senior professionals do international testing and methodology work.

## Key Concepts
- **Test participant**: not "subject". You're testing the thing, not the person.
- **Discount usability testing**: Nielsen's term for this simple, informal, small-sample approach.
- **Think aloud**: the participant narrates what they look at, try, and think.
- **Big Honkin' Test**: the formal, expensive, rarely done alternative (see ch03).
- **De facto user advocate**: the likely reader. They care about usability, have little budget, and do it in "copious spare time."

## Mental Models
- **Analytics tell you *what*; testing tells you *why*.** Long time on a page could mean great content or total confusion, and the numbers can't distinguish them. Krug: given perfect analytics or one hour with one user who thinks aloud and can be probed, "I'd take the one user every time."
- **Improve, not prove.** If someone demands statistical proof, the method was never trying to provide it.
- **Watching users is like travel**: it broadens you permanently.

## Anti-patterns
- **Testing only once, near the end.** This is the most common practice and the least useful one.
- **"Get it out the door, tweak later."** A lack of time is plausible but usually untrue as a reason not to test.
- **Reluctance to show unfinished work.** "We already know it has problems." You're there for the problems you *don't* know about.
- **Using this method where people can be hurt.** It makes things *easier* to use, not *foolproof*. Life-or-death systems need rigorous large-sample quantitative studies.
- **Faking neutrality to push an agenda.** Krug's hunch is that this is the main way amateur testing makes things worse.

## Worked Example: the 15-minute live demo
In his workshops Krug picks an attendee's site, spends a few minutes inventing a plausible task (on a health site, for example, "book an appointment"), and runs an unrehearsed 15-minute test with a volunteer. Across about fifty of these the outcome is always the same:
- The participant enjoys it and gets applause.
- The site owner scribbles notes the whole time and asks for the recording. In one case a single resulting change was estimated to save $100,000 a year by getting more customers onto online billing.
- Everyone else thinks "I could do that."
The lesson is that **there's nothing to it, and it always works**, whatever the site and whoever the participant.

## Key Takeaways
1. Testing = watching actual use. Opinions and recollections are a different method.
2. DIY testing is qualitative. Its goal is insight for improvement, not proof.
3. Your product has usability problems, and the serious ones are obvious to outsiders.
4. Fix the obvious problems first. Most teams never get past them.
5. Use analytics for *what* and testing for *why*.
6. If you can afford a professional, hire one. If not, do it yourself; doing it badly beats not doing it.
7. You can do this. "It's not rocket surgery."
