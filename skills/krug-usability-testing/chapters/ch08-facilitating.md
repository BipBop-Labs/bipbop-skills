# Chapter 8: Mind reading made easy

## Core Idea
The facilitator is both **tour guide** (keeps things moving) and **therapist** (keeps the participant verbalizing while staying neutral). **Think-aloud is the "secret sauce"**: it lets you see the product through the eyes of someone who knows less than you.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Two roles**:
  - **Tour guide**: tells them what to do, keeps them moving and happy, and does *not* answer questions about the sights.
  - **Therapist**: gets them to externalize their thoughts. You want to see the **thought balloons**, especially those holding **"?" or "!"** (confusion, puzzlement, frustration). When a balloon isn't visible, ask "What are you thinking?"
- **The session timeline (a 50-minute "therapist's hour")**:

| Segment | Minutes | Purpose |
|---|---|---|
| Pre-test prep | 60, once per day | Test the recorder and sharing, enlarge the cursor, silence interruptions, bookmark pages, try the product, reset data and history, brief the greeter |
| Welcome | 4 | **Read the script verbatim**: we test the site, not you; think aloud; honest reactions won't hurt us; I may not answer questions; recording consent; observers are watching the screen |
| Questions | 2 | A few easy questions: occupation, weekly internet hours, kinds of sites, favorite sites |
| Home page tour | 3 | "Tell me what you make of it… whose site, what you can do here, what it's for." Scrolling is OK, **no clicking yet** |
| Tasks | 35 | Hand over each scenario **and read it aloud word-for-word**. Minimal interruption |
| Probing | 5 | Check in with the observers first, then ask the deferred "why" questions |
| Wrap-up | 5 | Thanks, their questions, incentive, escort out |
| Between sessions | 10 | Stop and save the recording, reset the machine, jot notes, consider adjusting tasks |

- **Why read the script verbatim ("Don't improvise")**: even after twenty years, whenever Krug ad-libs, "odds are 50-50" he says something misleading, like the words "opinions" or "feedback". Small wording edits for naturalness are fine if you then say them the same way every time. Delivery: eye contact every few sentences (print it in large type), don't mumble, don't rush or drag, and avoid both monotone and melodrama.
- **The opening questions have three functions**: they warm up talking; they show you're actually listening (ask real follow-ups, and ask for an explanation if you don't understand); and they give you what you need to **grade on a curve**: occupation, computer and Web savvy, and, from the Home page tour, domain knowledge.
- **Home page tour**: a realistic task everyone does on arrival anyway, *figuring out what the site is*, verbalized. It is not an opinion prompt. If they click anyway: "For right now, I just want you to stick to the Home page. Could you go back?"
- **When to move to the next task** (four checks):
  1. **Did they complete it?** If they only *think* they did, ask them to try another way, which usually reveals the mistake.
  2. **Are they miserable?** The emotional arc runs *optimism → thought → puzzlement/confusion → frustration/anger → resignation/self-blame*. **"It's not a crash test."** You learn less from a miserable user. Always err toward the participant's feelings.
  3. **What's the time budget?**
  4. **Are you still learning?** When you stop expecting to learn anything, **let them go a little longer**, because about half the time something useful happens in the overtime.
  - Transition line: "That's great. Very helpful. I want to move **us** along, since **we**'ve got more to do." The *us/we* avoids implying they failed.
- **Probing**: during tasks, ask only for minor clarifications ("Do you mean the ___ over here?"). Note deeper whys ("Didn't notice left nav", "Chose 2nd link. Why?") for later. At probe time, ask whether they noticed X and why they chose Y; ask them to redo a task another way or from a different starting point; take them directly to pages they didn't reach; follow up on their feature suggestions. Usually those collapse into "I guess I wouldn't really use it," but a real bright idea is unmistakable because the whole room lights up.
- **Keep 'em talking, the prompting rule**: not a fixed silence timeout, but "**If you're not entirely sure you know what the user is thinking, ask.**" If they're clearly reading or confidently progressing, let them be. Participants don't notice being asked dozens of times. Vary it with "What are you looking at?" and "What are you doing?"
- **Stay neutral**: no clues, even subtle ones; answer questions with questions; never express opinions or agree with theirs; keep a poker face, ideally "consistently somewhat pleased." The worst case is a facilitator with an agenda (wanting the design to succeed or fail). Be aware of your bias or the tests lose credibility.
- **"Things a therapist would say"**:

| When this happens | Say this |
|---|---|
| You're not absolutely sure what they're thinking | "What are you thinking?" / "What are you looking at?" / "What are you doing now?" |
| Something seems to surprise them ("Oh", "Hmm" on a new page) | "Is that what you expected to happen?" |
| They're fishing for a clue ("Should I use the ___?") | "What would you do if you were at home?" (wait) "Then why don't you go ahead and try that?" / "What would you do if I wasn't here?" / "I'd like you to do whatever you'd normally do." |
| A comment and you don't know what triggered it | "Was there something in particular that made you think that?" |
| They worry they're not giving you what you need | "No, this is very helpful." / "This is exactly what we need." |
| They ask how something works | "What do you think?" / "How do you think it would work?" / "I can't answer that right now, because we need to know what you would do when you don't have somebody around to answer questions for you. But if you still want to know when we're done, I'll be glad to answer it then." |
| They've wandered away from the task | "What are you trying to do now?" |

  Also allowed: **acknowledgment tokens** ("uh huh", "OK", meaning *understood*, not *agreed*: "OK", not "OK!!!"); **paraphrasing** ("So you're saying the boxes at the bottom are hard to read?"); **clarifying for observers** ("The list over here on the right?").
- **Ethics: participants should leave in no worse shape than they entered.** They may stop anytime and still get paid. Watch their comfort and occasionally *offer* to stop. Protect privacy: no last names, no faces, recordings under your control and deleted when no longer needed, clips carrying a no-redistribution warning with personal data redacted, indiscreet remarks cut, and **never distribute clips of employee participants**. Academic settings may require IRB review, though informal tests often qualify for exemption.
- **Tough customers** (slow, silent, quiet, fast, nonstop, know-it-all, the rare wacko): be polite but firm. "Good. [pause] OK. [transition] We've got a lot to cover, so I'm going to ask you to…" Be persistent and a bit ruthless, since their time is paid for. Be patient with relapses, because hopeless-seeming participants sometimes turn out valuable. If someone is clearly unqualified, end early with a plausible excuse, thank them, and pay them.

## Key Concepts
- **Think-aloud protocol**, **thought balloon**, **acknowledgment token**, **poker face**, **Designated Greeter**, **50-minute hour**.
- **Test room kit**: your own laptop (so settings stay put), an external monitor and keyboard, a plain mouse (no trackpads or trackballs), a good USB mic (audio carries the participant's "body language"), VOIP for observers with a speakerphone as backup, and an **enlarged cursor**.

## Mental Models
- **The facilitator as therapist**: externalize their thinking, don't influence it, repeat the same few phrases, and carry ethical responsibility.
- **Audio beats video.** Good audio conveys emotion, and a face camera ("SquirmCam") mostly distracts.
- **Self-reported satisfaction is worthless at this scale.** People who nearly cried rate the site 6 out of 7, possibly out of politeness toward a host or something like Stockholm syndrome. That's why DIY tests skip entrance and exit questionnaires.

## Anti-patterns
- Letting participants read scenarios silently, which leads to misreadings and wasted time.
- Interrupting tasks for "why" questions, which breaks their flow and risks giving clues.
- Using the words "opinion" or "feedback" in instructions.
- Helping a struggling participant.
- Pushing a participant to misery "for the data".
- Choosing as facilitator someone who dislikes people, listens badly, is impatient, pushes their own ideas, or has a design agenda they can't set aside.

## Worked Example: handling a clue request
Participant: *"Should I click 'Plans' or 'Pricing'?"*
Facilitator: *"What would you do if you were at home?"*, then waits.
Participant: *"Probably Pricing."*
Facilitator: *"Then why don't you go ahead and try that?"*
If they later ask how billing works: *"How do you think it would work?"* Then, if pressed, give the script's "I can't answer that right now… glad to answer it when we're done."
The note taken for probing: *"Hesitated between Plans/Pricing. Why?"*

## Key Takeaways
1. Read the script verbatim, with warmth.
2. Start every web test with a 3-minute Home page tour: "what do you make of it?", not "what do you think of it?"
3. Read each scenario aloud while handing it over.
4. Ask "What are you thinking?" whenever you aren't sure what they're thinking.
5. Stay neutral: questions back, no opinions, poker face.
6. Move on when the task is done, they're miserable, time is short, or learning stops (after a little overtime).
7. Save the whys for the probe, and ask observers for their questions first.
8. They leave in no worse shape than they came, and their privacy is protected.
