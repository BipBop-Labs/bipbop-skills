# Chapter 10: Debriefing 101

## Core Idea
The lunch debrief produces two things: **a list of the most serious problems observed**, and **a list of the ones you commit to fix before next month**. Focus ruthlessly on the worst problems first.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Maxim: "Focus ruthlessly on a small number of the most important problems."** The logic, as a syllogism:
  1. All sites have usability problems.
  2. All organizations have limited resources to fix them.
  3. You'll always have more problems than resources.
  4. It's easy to get distracted by less serious, easier problems, so the worst ones persist.
  5. **Therefore, you must be intensely focused on fixing the most serious problems first.** Otherwise the worst ones will still be there in a month, and in six months.
- **Severity** is a judgment call on two axes:
  - **How many people** will hit it?
  - **How bad** is it for those who do: a real blocker (can't complete a transaction) or just an inconvenience?
  - Many people with big trouble is a no-brainer. The hard calls are **corner cases** (very damaging but rare) and **ubiquitous nuisances** (common but minor).
  - In practice, "if you actually watch people use your site, you'll know which problems are bad."
- **Entry rule**: if possible, only people who attended at least one session may join the debrief. It's the price of having a say, it drives attendance, and it prevents opinion-based "religious debates." Don't get fired enforcing it.
- **Format**: about an hour, as soon as possible after the sessions, **over good food ("get the good pizza")**.
- **Running it, in 8 steps**:
  1. Explain the process: "From your lists we'll choose the ten most serious problems, prioritize them, and agree which we'll commit to fixing in the next month."
  2. Everyone picks their three most serious from their notes.
  3. Go around the room reading them out ("I had ___, too" for duplicates).
  4. Write everything on an easel pad (big enough to see the whole list; whiteboards run out of room) with space between items for variations.
  5. Choose the **top ten**. Voting is optional. It's fine to say "Sounds like these are the top ten," check them off, and wait for objections.
  6. Rewrite them as a **rank-ordered list**, worst first, leaving room for fix notes.
  7. **Work down the list without skipping any**, briefly discussing how to fix each **within the next month**, with fixes as simple as possible (ch11).
  8. **Stop when the month's fixing capacity is committed.**
- **Pre-written ground rules on the pad**: *Stick to what you observed. Focus on the most serious problems. Objective: a list of problems we'll fix in the next month.*
- **Facilitating it**: it's your meeting and **not a democracy**; you're presumably the one who knows the most about usability. Have the recordings on a laptop to settle disputes. Pull people back to evidence: "Are you saying that because of something you saw in the tests?" "Did anybody in the tests have that problem?" Acknowledge every contribution, with no belittling.
- **The "Project Overlord" objection** ("that goes away when the big redesign ships"): be skeptical, because big projects slip, shrink, or die, and users suffer in the meantime. Ask: "**What's the smallest change we can make right now that will at least smooth over this problem for most people?**"
- **The small, non-honkin' report**: an email that takes **≤2 minutes to read and ≤30 minutes to write**, in bullets covering what was tested, the tasks, and the problems you'll fix this month, plus where the recordings are and when the next round is.

## Key Concepts
- **Top ten**: the shortlist of the most serious observed problems.
- **Corner case / ubiquitous nuisance**: the two hard severity quadrants.
- **Religious debate**: an opinion fight untethered from observation.

## Mental Models
- **Observation over opinion.** Everything in the room must trace back to something someone *saw*.
- **Commitment, not catalog.** The output is a fix plan sized to capacity, not an inventory.
- **Minor issues aren't banned.** Fix typos and nits on your own list. The debrief exists to aim *finite* resources at the worst first.

## Anti-patterns
- Debriefing days later, when memories have faded.
- Letting non-observers argue from opinion.
- Skipping a top-ten problem because it's hard. Do *something* about each one.
- Deferring to a future redesign.
- Writing a long report instead of the short email.
- Losing sight of the output, whatever the method (sticky notes on boards are fine), which is **a commitment to fix the worst problems**.

## Key Takeaways
1. Debrief the same day. Attending a session is the entry ticket.
2. Everyone brings their top three, and the facilitator distills a ranked top ten.
3. Rank severity by how many people it hits and how badly.
4. Go down the list in order, skip nothing, and choose the smallest fix for each.
5. Stop when this month's capacity is spoken for.
6. Keep the debate on observed evidence, and run the meeting firmly.
7. Summarize in a two-minute email.
