# Chapter 9: Make it a spectator sport

## Core Idea
Get as many people as possible (developers, designers, managers, executives) to **watch tests live**. Seeing is believing. Watching converts skeptics and permanently teaches people that **users aren't like them**.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Maxim: "Make it a spectator sport."**
- **Seeing is believing.** Two changes happen to observers:
  1. They stop being skeptical about testing and usually become enthusiasts.
  2. The subtler and more important one: they realize **users aren't just like them. In fact, they're not all like *anybody*.** This permanently changes their relationship to users.
  - The effect is **much stronger live than on recordings**, the way watching a game live beats watching the replay, and the shared group experience adds to it.
- **Getting people to come**: pick a slow day of the week and month; send a "save the date" two weeks ahead naming what's being tested, a tickler a few days before, and a reminder the day before; show stakeholders what's in it for them (**attending gives them a voice in the debrief** where their pet problems get decided); "trick" executives into "just dropping by" for morale, since VPs have cancelled meetings to keep watching; **provide quality snacks**.
- **The observer's job**: watch, learn, and take notes; **after each session, write down the three most serious problems from that session**; pass questions to the facilitator; enjoy the snacks; come to the debrief.
- **Observation room setup**: a conference room (or a training room or auditorium for larger crowds) that is **not adjacent to the test room**, so group laughter or groans can't reach the participant. It needs a screen-share client, a projector or large screen big and bright enough for detail, powered speakers (audio matters as much as in the test room), a backup speakerphone with the test room's number, and snacks that follow local custom.
- **Hall Monitor**: someone attending anyway who **isn't intimidated by bosses**. They hand out observer instructions, the script, and the scenarios; troubleshoot audio and video and call the facilitator if it's stuck; head off long off-topic chats; point phone-takers to the door with a smile; and after each session, remind everyone to write their top three and collect lists from people who can't attend the debrief.

## Key Concepts
- **Observer instructions**: a one-page handout. Take notes (especially confusion and failures); write the top three problems after each session; come to the debrief (free lunch); write down questions for the participant; stay as long as you can; don't distract others (the movie-theater rule).
- **Top Three Usability Problems sheet**: three lines for each of the three participants.

## Mental Models
- **Conversion by demonstration.** Watching tests is the mechanism that makes "fanatics", which ch13 relies on.
- **Remote viewing only for people who truly can't attend** (another city, say). People who are on-site but watch from their desks will multitask and miss both the value and the group experience.

## Anti-patterns
- **Observers in the test room.** Participants cope, but observers can't stay quiet: managers ask marketing questions, developers solicit opinions on their pet feature. If it's unavoidable: introduce them; be silent with phones off; answer nothing unless the facilitator asks, and then briefly; poker face with no sighing; no coaching, nodding, or grinning.
- An adjacent observation room.
- Bad audio or a small screen, so people drift into email.
- Relying on recordings instead of attendance. "In the universe I live in, it almost never happens" that people watch them later.

## FAQ Worth Remembering: hurt feelings
Watching users struggle with your work is rarely a serious problem. Testing usually suggests the solution too, sometimes for a long-standing problem. **Soften the first exposure by testing competitors' sites first**, where nobody is invested. People are really troubled only when testing comes **too late to fix anything**. If egos are bruised, say at the debrief: "We saw some problems, but by and large the thing worked remarkably well, and the problems are quite fixable."

## Key Takeaways
1. Maximize live attendance, especially by decision-makers.
2. Market it: save-the-date, reminders, a voice in the debrief, snacks.
3. Every observer writes their top three problems after every session.
4. Put observers in a separate, non-adjacent room with good audio and a big screen.
5. Appoint a Hall Monitor.
6. Remote viewing is for people who can't come, not for people who don't feel like it.
