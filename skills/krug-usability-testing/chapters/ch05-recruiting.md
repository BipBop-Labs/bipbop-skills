# Chapter 5: Recruit loosely and grade on a curve

## Core Idea
Who you test with matters less than people think. Find people who roughly reflect your audience but don't obsess over it. **Three participants per round is enough**, because more rounds beat bigger rounds.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Krug's First Law of Usability Testing: "Testing with one user is 100% better than testing with none."**
- **Maxim: "Recruit loosely and grade on a curve."**
  - *Recruit loosely*: try to find people who reflect your audience, but don't get hung up on it.
  - *Grade on a curve*: when a participant struggles, ask "**Would our users have that problem?** Or was it only a problem because this person didn't know the jargon or the subject matter, which we're sure our users would know?"
- **Why "test only real users" is overrated**:
  - Many of the most serious problems (navigation, layout, visual hierarchy) have **nothing to do with domain knowledge**. Almost anybody will hit them.
  - **Your audience is more diverse than you think.** Beginners without domain knowledge still need to use the product, like first-time buyers on an insurance site.
  - **Domain experts don't know what you think they know.** A term the designers swore every real-estate agent used made a real agent ask "What's this?"
  - **Outsiders have an emperor's-new-clothes effect**: they reveal what insiders take for granted. Krug: "I'd rather have one articulate outsider with reasonable common sense who's comfortable talking than ten 'real' users who are tense, quirky, etc."
  - Domain knowledge *does* matter in some cases. An industrial-crane order form with fields like "Height Under Boom" needs people who know cranes. When you need actual users, get them.
  - **Drift over time**: early rounds find problems anyone would hit, so recruit loosely. As the product matures, lean toward actual users, but still include **one "ringer"** (outsider) per round.
- **Three is enough**, and the question is framed differently from the research debate. The research asks how many users find *most* problems ("5 users find 85%"). Your question is how many find **as many problems as you can fix**. Reasons for three:
  1. The first three users hit many of the most significant problems for the tasks tested.
  2. Three are easier to find than more.
  3. **More rounds matter more than more users per round**, and small rounds make more rounds possible.
  4. You can test and debrief the same day.
  5. It's easier to get observers to come.
  6. **Tedium factor**: from user 4 on, observers start snacking, checking voicemail, and chatting.
  7. More users produce more notes than anyone can process, many of them nits, so you "can't see the forest for the trees."
  8. A pile of problems is dispiriting and makes triage a project of its own.

## Techniques
- **Outsourcing**: recruiting is **the only part of the process Krug would outsource**. Focus-group or market-research recruiters define the profile, find, screen, schedule, and remind participants. (2009 cost: about $100 or less per participant, more for rare profiles.)
- **Where to look (the Willie Sutton rule: go where they congregate)**: senior centers and libraries for seniors; user groups, SIGs, and trade shows for product users (you can even test *at* a show); a Home page link or an entry/exit pop-up for your own site's visitors; friends, family, and neighbors for "just anyone"; classifieds sites like Craigslist.
- **Internal staff**: usually they *know too much*. Never use people who build, support, sell, train on, or document the product. People from other divisions, admin staff, reception, finance, or HR can work. **For an intranet, new employees are perfect**: eager, with domain knowledge, and they *are* the target audience.
- **The source that never works**: marketing's offer of a customer list. Some wrinkle always appears, whether it's "don't contaminate our customers" or privacy.
- **Invitation**: state date, duration (~1 hour), location, and the qualifying profile. **Ask for replies by email, not phone**, or you'll drown in voicemail.
- **Screening call**: check availability; verify qualifications (some people stretch the truth for money); set expectations (about an hour, using a website, the screen and voice are recorded but not the face); explain compensation; **judge whether they'd be comfortable thinking aloud and are articulate**; book a slot.
- **Follow-up**: a confirmation email right after the call (directions, parking, the room, a day-of emergency contact, the NDA if you use one), then a reconfirmation call a few days before.
- **Incentives**: from about $50 for average users to several hundred for specialists such as cardiologists. Pay **slightly above the going rate**, which signals you value them and improves punctuality and eagerness. Gift cards are the least hassle; cash and checks bring receipts, tax IDs, and accounting. Some people (government employees, for instance) can't accept payment, so send a gracious **letter**, not an email. Mementos work for some.
- **Heir and a spare**: always have a **standby participant** on call for the whole morning. It can be anyone nearby (another department or building), or an "actual user" reachable for remote testing when the profile is specific. A no-show sends observers back to their desks, and they may not return.

## Key Concepts
- **Domain knowledge**: expertise in the subject field. London cabbies' "The Knowledge" is the extreme example.
- **False positive**: a problem that only a non-target participant would have.
- **Ringer**: a deliberate outsider in the round.
- **Screener**: the qualifying phone conversation.

## Mental Models
- **You will miss serious problems in any single round, and that's a certainty, not a risk.** The fix is more rounds, not more users.
- **Participants are single-use per product.** Afterwards they know too much. Pass good ones to other teams testing other products.

## Anti-patterns
- Obsessing over a perfect demographic match and ending up testing less often.
- Testing colleagues who know the product.
- Relying on marketing's customer list.
- Giving out a phone number in the invitation.
- Having no standby participant.
- Running more than three sessions in a day and drowning in nits.

## Key Takeaways
1. One user beats zero. Three per round is the default.
2. Recruit loosely early and tighten toward real users as the product matures. Always include one ringer.
3. Grade on a curve: discount problems caused only by the participant's missing domain knowledge.
4. Recruiting is the one step worth outsourcing.
5. Screen for articulateness and comfort thinking aloud, not only for profile.
6. Pay a bit above market, confirm twice, and keep a standby on call.
