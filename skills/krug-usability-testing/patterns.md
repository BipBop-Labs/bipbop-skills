# Krug Usability Testing Patterns: techniques

## Monthly testing round
**When to use**: as the backbone of any product team's testing practice.
**How**: fix a recurring date (e.g., the third Thursday), test three users in the morning, debrief over lunch, and send a short email. Test whatever is ready that day.
**Trade-offs**: you'll miss some serious problems in each round, and more rounds compensate. The organizer carries 1–3 days of prep.
→ Ch 3

## Napkin test
**When to use**: for concepts, sketches, titles, and early page ideas, before building anything. This is the book's main discovery-stage technique.
**How**: approach almost anyone and ask "What do you make of this? What do you think it's supposed to be?" Listen, and optionally probe unclear terms. If they describe your intent, keep going. If not, you've found a problem for free.
**Trade-offs**: tests comprehension only, not task completion or desirability.
→ Ch 4

## Competitor testing
**When to use**: before designing, when the team is new to testing, or to engage management.
**How**: run the full process on other products with your key tasks, possibly the same task across 2–3 products. The debrief discusses lessons, not fixes.
**Trade-offs**: gives you no fix list for your own product. Low ego risk makes it a gentle first round.
→ Ch 4, 9

## Wireframe navigation test
**When to use**: when information architecture and labels exist but visuals don't.
**How**: "How would you find ___?" "What would you expect if you clicked this?" Combine it with other tests in one session.
**Trade-offs**: short, since there isn't much to do with a wireframe. It catches org-chart structures and bad names.
→ Ch 4

## Comp walkthrough
**When to use**: when visual designs of key pages or templates exist.
**How**: lead them through the pages starting at the Home page and ask for a narrative of each.
**Trade-offs**: checks whether visuals obscure how pages work, not whether flows succeed.
→ Ch 4

## Loose recruiting with grading on a curve
**When to use**: every round.
**How**: roughly match your audience with one ringer per round. When someone fails, ask whether your real users would have hit it too.
**Trade-offs**: risks false positives from missing domain knowledge. Tighten toward real users as the product matures, and for domain-heavy tasks.
→ Ch 5

## Task-to-scenario conversion
**When to use**: before every round.
**How**: list 5–10 user goals and pick them by criticality, worry, and support or analytics signals. Write each as "You are… you need to…" with required data, **without on-screen words**. Ban search unless you're testing it. Pilot, then print unnumbered, one per sheet.
**Trade-offs**: more context helps comprehension but costs reading time, so trim everything non-essential.
→ Ch 6

## Scripted facilitation with think-aloud
**When to use**: every moderated session.
**How**: read the script verbatim; ask a few warm-up questions; do the Home page tour; read each scenario aloud; prompt whenever you're unsure what they think; stay neutral with the therapist phrases; defer the whys to probing.
**Trade-offs**: the script feels stiff at first, so practice it aloud four or five times.
→ Ch 8

## Home page tour
**When to use**: at the start of every web session, and as a standalone quick check.
**How**: "Look at this page and tell me what you make of it: what strikes you, whose site it is, what you can do here, what it's for. Scroll, but don't click yet." Allow about 3 minutes.
**Trade-offs**: tests orientation only. That's also what catches wrong-foot problems.
→ Ch 8, 12

## Deferred probing
**When to use**: whenever you're curious *why* during a task.
**How**: jot a note ("Chose 2nd link. Why?"). After the tasks, get the observers' questions first, then probe: did you notice X? why Y? try another way? go to page Z.
**Trade-offs**: memory fades a little. The alternative, interrupting mid-task, breaks their flow and leaks clues.
→ Ch 8

## Spectator-sport observation
**When to use**: every round.
**How**: a separate, non-adjacent room with a big screen, good speakers, and snacks. Aggressive invitations. A Hall Monitor. Every observer writes their top three per session.
**Trade-offs**: needs a room and food. Pays back as team conversion and buy-in.
→ Ch 9

## Top-ten debrief
**When to use**: the same day as the sessions.
**How**: attendees only; everyone reads their top three; the facilitator distills a ranked top ten; walk down it without skipping, choose the least-you-can-do fix for each, and stop at capacity.
**Trade-offs**: not a democracy. The facilitator must run it firmly on observed evidence.
→ Ch 10

## Least-you-can-do tweak loop
**When to use**: fixing any observed problem.
**How**: make the simplest change, check it, try it louder, try a different tweak, check for side effects. Consider removing something first.
**Trade-offs**: can accumulate patches ("kludges"), which still beat leaving the problem in place. Deep, strategic problems still need a separate conversation.
→ Ch 11

## Make it shout
**When to use**: when users miss something important.
**How**: increase contrast, size, or position well beyond what feels necessary, while keeping the design attractive.
**Trade-offs**: designers resist. Overuse makes everything shout, so reserve it for the few things users must notice.
→ Ch 12

## Cheap fix verification
**When to use**: when you're unsure a tweak worked.
**How**: first just look at it. Then a hallway test with anyone, or 1–2 unmoderated remote users on the same task, or an A/B test. Re-test major changes next round.
**Trade-offs**: each step up costs more effort.
→ Ch 11, 14

## Remote moderated session
**When to use**: for hard-to-find profiles, distant users, Agile cadence, or a standby participant.
**How**: the same script. Test the connection during the confirmation call. The participant shares their own screen with a zero-install tool. Observers still gather in one room.
**Trade-offs**: ~80% of the benefit for ~70% of the effort, with less control and more clarifying. Do it only after ~3 in-person rounds.
→ Ch 14

## Unmoderated remote test
**When to use**: quick one-off questions and fix retests.
**How**: a URL plus 1–2 short tasks plus a participant count on a testing service, with think-aloud recordings back in about a day.
**Trade-offs**: no probing, so it's weak on *why*.
→ Ch 14

## Demonstration over persuasion
**When to use**: when you need management or stakeholder commitment.
**How**: get them to watch live sessions, give them a voice in the debrief, and skip the ROI decks.
**Trade-offs**: slower to start, but durable and recession-proof.
→ Ch 13
